OIKOS: a return to the roots?

Well before it was the name of a yoghurt brand, oikos was the basic unit of society in ancient Greece: a household. The management of an oikos was oikonomia, which gives us the word "economy". The study of a household in relation to its physical and human environment is "ecology". More recently, oikos has named an individual's social circle.

That centrality still holds. People manage their means and needs within economic systems, integrated in a web of social networks, and rooted in a local physical environment. They operate in an ecosystem, no points for guessing that word's root.

Yet our current frameworks do not place households within any such ecosystem. Homo economicus is modeled as a rational agent allocating consumption and leisure to maximize individual utility, a simplification shown inaccurate again and again.

We continually hear of rising income and wealth inequality, of marginalized populations, of unequal access to the traditional engines of development: education, jobs, housing. We are reminded daily of environmental fragility, and that those least equipped to confront it are most affected. Individual wellbeing is multifaceted, and clearly decoupling from material gain, even as economies reach new heights of aggregate prosperity.

Applied development work from community organizations, community colleges, and workforce boards elevates the foundational role that basic needs, social networks, and community engagement play in giving households the means to manage and grow. Some of the ecosystem's fundamental supports and on-ramps are social and human, not purely economic.

Part of the reason models omit this is ideological. Part is difficulty, since evidence and proof require good data and real computing power. Yet the models are nonetheless not yielding equitable, sustainable, resilient development. So how do we build development frameworks rooted in these multi-dimensional factors while still promoting prosperity?

OIKOS is a research program built to fill that gap. Rooted in rigorous economic and social science and in the lived experience of communities, new evidence-based frameworks centered on households' ecosystems can show a way forward.
