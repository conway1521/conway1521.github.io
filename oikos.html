OIKOS Economics

A return to the roots?

Well before being the name of a popular yoghurt brand, oikos represented the basic unit of society in ancient Greece: a household. The management of an oikos was termed oikonomia, which gives us the word "economy". The study of a household in relation to its physical and human environments is "ecology". More recently, oikos has been used in sociology to define an individual's social circle.

The centrality of this ancient term in households' economic life remains relevant today: people manage their means and needs within economic systems, integrated in the web of social networks, and rooted in their local physical environment. They operate in an ecosystem (no points for guessing this word's root).

Yet our current economic frameworks do not factor households within any such ecosystem. Homo economicus is modelled as a rational agent that optimally allocates consumption and leisure given constraints, so as to maximise individual "utility". This has been shown again and again to be an inaccurate simplification.

On the news we continually hear of rising income and wealth inequality, of disenfranchised and marginalised populations, and of unequal access to the traditional engines of development: education, jobs, housing. We are reminded daily of the fragility of our environment and of the drastic consequences that changes are having on livelihoods, especially as those least equipped to confront them are most impacted. Individual wellbeing is multifaceted, but is quite clearly decoupling from material gain. All the while, our economies reach new heights of aggregated prosperity.

Applied economic development work from community-based organisations, community colleges, workforce boards, and other direct service providers elevates the foundational role that basic needs, social networks, and community engagement play in providing households the means to manage their livelihoods and grow. Access to these is especially crucial for overcoming barriers to inclusive, shared prosperity. In other words, some of the ecosystem's fundamental elements providing support and onramps to economic development are social and human, not purely economic.

Part of the reason why past and current economic models do not factor much of this (if any) is ideological. Another part is sheer difficulty. Empirical evidence and mathematical proof simply require good data and significant computing power. Yet these models are nonetheless not successful in yielding equitable, sustainable, and resilient development. So how can we build new economic development frameworks rooted in these multi-dimensional factors while still promoting prosperity?

OIKOS is a research programme developed to fill this gap. Rooted in rigorous economic and social science research as well as the lived experience of communities, new evidence-based frameworks centred around households' ecosystems can show a way forward.
