About

At Radius Intelligence I designed and built the data department end to end: economics, models, pipelines, and product. The platform reconciles federal statistics, commercial postings, training and credential records, fiscal signals, and private employer data into one view across every US occupation and geography, on a compliant multi-tenant layer of 220 datasets and millions of records that grows every time a new client connects, and the two digital twins below sit on top of it, one that takes an employer's raw HR export through to forecasts and scenarios and one that models a state's workforce as stocks and flows that have to balance.

My research runs on one idea, that economies behave as ecosystems and that policy fails when it treats the parts separately. My PhD at Université Paris 8 formalizes that over place and tests it on EU regional data, where regions sort into a four-tier ladder and the pandemic shock moved them down that ladder four times as often as up. An applied strand measures the same idea in labor markets, decomposing the national wage bill by role, task, and skill so that AI exposure becomes a question of dollars rather than index units.

Two of the things I have built are live and open to anyone: the Open Inequality Atlas, a versioned openly licensed panel of household net-wealth inequality that now holds 10,783 observations across 213 countries, and the NED dashboard, an assessment of economic development across California's regions built on indicators that start from what mayors, community colleges, workforce boards, and employers see on the ground.

Before Radius I led workforce and economic insights at Jobs for the Future, where I built a national green-workforce readiness dashboard, advised a 500,000-person placement initiative, and ran an analysis of how AI is transforming jobs across the US labor market. Before that I researched the distributional effects of fiscal policy at OFCE and the Banque de France, work that continues as a co-authored paper. My master's thesis at Sciences Po was published in the University of Oxford Social Macroeconomics working paper series, and the model is available in QuantEcon.

Outside work I build and play guitars, mix songs, and make olive oil.

Systems and tools

Live

Open Inequality AtlasOpen data, live

Wealth inequality data is scattered across mutually incompatible sources, each using a different unit, methodology, and license, so a comparable cross-country panel is a research project before it is an input to one.

The Atlas closes that gap into a single versioned openly licensed panel of household net-wealth inequality, currently 10,783 observations across 213 countries from 1800 to 2025, with every row tagged by source priority and comparability tier so a user can decide how much reconciliation they are willing to accept rather than inheriting mine.

Launch the Atlas
NED dashboard, California regional analysisEconomics, policy, live

An interactive assessment of economic development across California's regions, on indicators that start from what mayors, community colleges, workforce boards, and employers see on the ground.

Rather than beginning from the indicators that happen to be collected, it begins from what regional actors say determines whether a household can get on and stay on, and then asks which of those things can be measured well enough to compare regions against each other. It is the three-pillar framework from my dissertation running on a real place.

Launch the dashboard
Wellbeing and macroeconomics explorerMacroeconomics, live, QuantEcon

Turns the SAGE model from my Oxford paper into something you can move the parameters on, so you can watch how the social psychology terms change what happens to a household during a shock.

The model is available in QuantEcon, the open source project for quantitative economics, computational lectures and tools in Python and Julia founded by Thomas Sargent and John Stachurski and used widely in teaching and research.

The Radius platform

The employer digital twin and the labor market digital twin are the ones to start with, and the rest are the smaller problems I had to solve to get those two running, each one making a longer chain possible. The code and the underlying data stay private, so what these describe is the problem, the approach, and where it landed, and every figure on the detail pages is built on synthetic data for illustration.

Employer digital twinData science, econometrics, product

An employer wants to know whether their turnover is bad, what is coming, and what to do about it, and their raw HR export answers none of that on its own.

The twin takes that export from upload to forecast and scenarios, with every number auditable back to the raw rows, which is what makes it usable in a room where somebody is going to be asked to defend it.

Read more
Labor market digital twinEconomics, policy, data science

States plan their workforce with statistics that lag by years and projections that arrive as black boxes, neither of which can be interrogated when the number turns out to be wrong.

The twin models the market as stocks and flows that must balance, meaning who enters, who leaves, demand built four separate ways, and the gap left over once the four are reconciled against each other.

Read more
Occupation resolverMachine learning, LLM systems, data engineering

Employers name the same job a hundred ways, and nothing downstream works until those titles collapse onto one taxonomy, so there is no benchmark, no forecast, and no comparison across employers.

The resolver fixes this once, for any title, through a ten-stage pipeline over three model layers, and both digital twins depend on it.

Read more
Data lakeData engineering, data science

Connecting datasets across the labor market takes a crosswalk onto the federal taxonomy, and the sources disagree on unit, grain, vintage, and license.

The lake reconciles 220 of them so that any one can be joined to any other, with a canon that fixes the disagreements once and a librarian layer that answers questions about what is in there.

Workforce blueprintsEconomics, data science

A new facility is planned and a region needs to know what it means, meaning how many workers, in which occupations, on what schedule.

Announcements give a headline headcount and staffing statistics describe yesterday's mix, so the blueprint has to be built rather than looked up.

Occupational opportunity scoringPolicy, economics

A workforce board has one real question, which is which occupations to back and what would actually move them.

This scores every occupation on five visible pillars and attaches levers only where the evidence supports them, always with a confidence band and never as a bare point, because a point estimate handed to a board becomes a target within a week.

Task-based labor marketEconomics, machine learning

The main measures of AI exposure disagree with each other, and claims about AI's effect on jobs have outrun anyone's ability to measure them.

This is an instrument rather than another estimate, a calibrated production function in which every parameter is disclosed, sourced, and swappable, paired with a research paper in progress.

Market signalsEconomics, data science

Official statistics arrive quarters late and decisions cannot wait for them.

This grades fifteen kinds of leading evidence, among them postings, layoff notices, and wage anomalies, by how much confidence each one deserves, and composes them into two flags whose logic stays readable to the person acting on them.
