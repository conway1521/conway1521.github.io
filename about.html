About

Regional development policy is not working. Despite decades of investment, inequality persists and the communities most in need are often left further behind. My work asks why and builds what comes next.

The problem does not seem to be lack of effort or funding. It is that we have treated economic development as a collection of isolated interventions when in reality, regional economies are ecosystems. Labour markets, housing, transport, education, employer needs, and social networks all interact. Policies that ignore these connections do not just underperform; they often make things worse.

My PhD at Université Paris 8 formalises this insight into a new framework. It examines how place-based conditions, human and social capital, and economic activity interact across EU regions. The goal is not just academic: it is to understand why well-intentioned policies fail, so we can design ones that do not.

At Radius Intelligence (RI), I am putting this thinking into practice: the ecosystem framing from the dissertation, built end to end and operational. As Head of data science and engineering, I run the department across economics, engineering, and data science. RI Technologies handles employer forecasting and workforce planning, RI Labs covers policy and labour market analysis, and the underlying data substrate stands as a product in its own right, covering every occupation across the US at every depth of geography. The result is a living system that helps regions anticipate shortages, match people to opportunities, and build resilience before shocks hit.

Previously, I led workforce and economic insights at Jobs for the Future, developing a national green workforce readiness dashboard, advising Verizon's initiative to place 500,000 under-represented individuals into quality careers, and analysing how AI is transforming jobs.

My master's thesis at Sciences Po (Fulbright Scholar) integrated social psychology into macroeconomic models to explore how personal wellbeing shapes economic decisions, published in the University of Oxford Social Macroeconomics series.
