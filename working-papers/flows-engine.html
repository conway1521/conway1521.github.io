Research Working paper

Where workers come from

Workforce policy is organized around training, on the understanding that an occupation short of people is an occupation short of graduates. This builds a full account of how people actually enter and leave every occupation in the United States, and finds that arrivals from education are the smallest of the inflows by a wide margin.

Working paper · public microdata · every number regenerates from a clean checkout.
01

The premise

Employment statistics report stocks precisely and frequently, and say nothing about the flows that produced them, so an occupation that is stable in headcount and an occupation that replaces a fifth of its people every year are recorded identically. The flows have been studied, and the literature that studies them has not met the literature that studies shortages. Neither has produced an account a workforce planner could open and read off, for a named occupation, how many people arrived from training, how many arrived from another occupation, how many left for another occupation and how many left work altogether.

This paper builds that account for 230 occupations, annually from 2009 to 2025, disaggregated by age band, with a standard error on every figure computed from the 160 replicate weights the Census Bureau publishes for the survey. The account balances exactly rather than approximately, because every person is counted once on each side of the identity from a single source, and nothing in it is imputed or calibrated to an external total.

02

The instrument

The obvious way to build the account does not work, and establishing that has to come first. Occupational mobility is normally measured by following a person across two interviews and comparing the occupation recorded on each. The Current Population Survey assigns occupation codes from written descriptions of what a person does, and two independent codings of the same unchanged job disagree in 48.1 percent of cases at the detailed level and in 31.8 percent across only twenty-three major groups.

That figure is measured on 114,491 people whose occupation the survey itself records as unchanged and who worked all fifty-two weeks of the reference year, with the person link validated against state of residence and sex, which disagree in 0.00 percent of cases, and against education, which disagrees in 12.6. A linked measure of annual mobility built on that foundation returns 50.0 percent, which is below the disagreement between two codings of a period during which nothing could have changed, so real movement sits invisible beneath the noise.

The March supplement avoids the problem for a specific reason. It asks in a single interview what a person does now and what they did last year, so both codes are assigned at one sitting from one respondent's description, their errors are correlated, and the correlation cancels when the difference between them is taken. The instrument that relies on recall is the one that works, and the disagreements themselves show why: they follow boundaries in the classification, elementary against secondary teachers, supervisors against the staff they supervise, generic against specific managerial categories, rather than the paths along which careers run.

03

Findings

01
Occupations are refilled from other occupations, at four to twelve times the rate they are refilled from education.
This holds for 98 percent of occupations. Aggregated across the whole matrix, arrivals from another occupation run 4.29 times arrivals from outside employment at the detailed level, 2.89 at minor group and 1.55 across the twenty-three major groups, where the classification cannot confuse a nurse with a physical therapist and the surviving flow is movement between broad occupational families.
02
Those arrivals occur at every age, so they are career movement rather than training under another name.
The obvious objection is that arrivals recorded as coming from another occupation are graduates who held a job while studying. If so they would concentrate among the young. For registered nurses the rate runs 3.1, 2.2, 3.4, 1.3 and 2.8 per hundred across the age bands from twenty-five upwards, which is flat, and only 42 to 55 percent of lateral arrivals land in the two youngest bands against roughly a third of the workforce being that age.
03
Most movement between occupations cancels.
Splitting the transition matrix into a symmetric part and a directional one separates two-way traffic from net reallocation, and symmetric misclassification cannot manufacture direction. Only 36.4 percent of gross lateral movement carries direction at the detailed level, 22.1 at minor group and 14.5 at major group. An occupation's stock is churned by movements several times larger than the change in its size, so a policy that raises arrivals works against departures of comparable magnitude.
04
The nursing shortage sits next to nursing rather than inside it.
Registered nurses have the lowest total departure rate of the large health and education occupations, at 9.4 per hundred a year. Nursing and home health aides lose 22.7, and the difference is concentrated in movement to other occupations rather than in departure from work.
04

What is next

What follows for policy is a question about the size of the lever rather than a recommendation. Training capacity acts on the smallest of an occupation's inflows, and the largest inflow is governed by whatever determines who can move into an occupation from another one: credential requirements, licensure, and the transferability of skills between adjacent occupations. Which of those binds, and for which occupations, is the question the account was built to make askable. A companion paper takes the same account to the geography, asking whether these patterns differ between states and measuring the interstate movement of workers by occupation.
