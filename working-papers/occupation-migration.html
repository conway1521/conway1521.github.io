Research Working paper

Where occupations move

Interstate migration is well documented for people and barely documented for occupations. A state deciding whether to train workers or recruit them needs to know how much of its workforce arrives from outside, in which occupations, and who those arrivals are. This measures all three across 479 occupations and nineteen years of census microdata, and tests the answers against an employer survey that had no part in building them.

Working paper · fully public data · every number regenerates from a clean checkout.
01

The premise

Roughly 2.3 per cent of employed Americans crossed a state line in a given year over this period, a figure stable enough that it conceals almost everything of interest. Within it, tool and die makers replenish two thirds of one per cent of their number from other states each year while sailors replenish close to a tenth. Any instrument aimed at labor mobility acts on that distribution rather than on its mean, and the distribution has not previously been measured at occupation detail across a panel of this length.

The migration literature treats occupation coarsely or not at all, from Molloy, Smith and Wozniak on the aggregate decline to Kennan and Walker on the individual decision, while the literature that does resolve occupation treats one occupation at a time. Between the two sits the object this paper builds: bilateral state-to-state flows by occupation, state and year, from 59.8 million person records across nineteen vintages, 2005 to 2024 with the 2020 gap, 479 harmonized occupation codes and 51 jurisdictions, with the zero corridors kept and design-based intervals on every reported share.

02

Why the elasticity cannot be estimated one occupation at a time

Flows are estimated by Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood, which keeps the origin-destination pairs where nobody moves rather than dropping them, and they are 92 per cent of the sample:

$$\mathbb{E}\big[\text{flow}_{o \to d}^{\,k t}\big] = \exp\!\Big(\eta \ln \tfrac{w_{dkt}}{w_{okt}} + \beta \ln \text{dist}_{od} + \gamma\, \text{contig}_{od} + \phi_{ot} + \psi_{dt} + \lambda_{kt} + \mu_{od}\Big)$$

Fitting a separate \( \eta_k \) per occupation, which is the natural specification and the one this project began with, returns a clean null that is an artifact of the design. Within a single occupation the wage differential is identified only from year-to-year movement in a survey cell mean, and a reliability calculation attributes 54 per cent of that movement to sampling noise, so a true elasticity of 0.5 would appear as 0.23 against standard errors that cannot detect it. Pooling occupations identifies \( \eta \) from variation across occupations inside one state pair and year, where reliability is 0.76, and it permits the origin-by-year and destination-by-year effects that absorb time-varying multilateral resistance. The wage response is real and small; the per-occupation null was a measurement failure rather than a finding.

03

What the paper establishes

01
How much of a workforce is imported varies thirteenfold.
Tool and die makers replenish 0.66 per cent of their number from other states each year and sailors 9.70 per cent, with confidence intervals at the two ends an order of magnitude apart. Occupations at the top either serve networks that span states, as pilots, air traffic controllers and aircraft mechanics do, or draw on a national market for scarce training, as physical scientists and physicians do. Occupations at the bottom are tied to a fixed local asset: a farm, a postal route, a stretch of highway, a municipal fire service. Licensure does not organize the distribution, and physicians and veterinarians sit near the top of it.
02
Migration delivers workers of one age, and no occupation imports experience.
Arrivals average 34.0 years of age, leavers average 34.0, and the workforce they join or leave averages 41.3. That arrivals and leavers are identical in age is a regularity rather than opposing tendencies cancelling: two thirds of state and occupation cells put them within two years of each other. Across every occupation with enough arrivals to measure, incomers are younger than the people already doing the job, from 2.9 years for waiters to 11.9 for aircraft mechanics and 11.0 for physicians. A state short of experienced workers cannot recruit its way out of it, at any wage, in any occupation, so training and early-career retention are the instruments that remain.
03
Pay is a weak lever and distance is a strong constraint.
The wage elasticity is 0.114, so a destination paying ten per cent more draws about one per cent more migration, and a state seeking ten per cent more arrivals through pay alone would have to raise the occupation's wage by something approaching ninety per cent. The distance elasticity is near −0.69 and, unlike the wage elasticity, it can be estimated occupation by occupation, where it is significant in 93 per cent of them and varies threefold: near zero for editors and chemists, who are recruited nationally, and near unity for roofers and printing press operators, for whom recruitment beyond neighboring states is largely wasted.
04
An employer survey agrees that workers follow jobs rather than pay.
Ten hypotheses were specified before the results were seen and all ten are reported, corrected for multiple testing. Three hold. The gravity residual tracks the location quotient in the Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, which is collected from employers and shares no data with the household microdata used here, and external dependence and net inflow both predict subsequent employment growth. The three wage tests fail, and they fail precisely rather than for want of power, returning coefficients of −0.0004, −0.0014 and +0.0001 against standard errors between 0.004 and 0.007. Two unrelated sources therefore agree that interstate migration tracks where employment is expanding and bears no detectable relationship to where pay is rising.
05
Turnover is not accumulation.
Occupations differ twentyfold in the share of their workforce crossing a state line each year in either direction, and almost none of that traffic accumulates. Aircraft pilots move 17.09 per cent of their workforce annually for a net balance of 0.000 per cent. In a high-turnover occupation every worker a state attracts is matched by one it loses, so retention rather than recruitment is the binding constraint, while in a low-turnover occupation the channel is too narrow to recruit through at all.
04

What is next

Departures from the gravity benchmark are large, stable across two decades and unexplained. They recover concentrations the model was never told about, placing Washington at 2.82 times its predicted intake of software developers, the District of Columbia at 3.64 times its predicted intake of lawyers and Michigan at 4.16 times its predicted intake of mechanical engineers, while South Carolina, Tennessee and Kentucky draw between 0.28 and 0.44 of their predicted software developers. Locating a departure is a different exercise from accounting for one. Occupational licensure compacts take effect between specific pairs of states on specific dates, which is variation of the kind that could account for part of them, and that is where the work goes next.
