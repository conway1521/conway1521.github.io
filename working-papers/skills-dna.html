Skills DNA of the economy

Roles, tasks, and skills are three equivalent decompositions of the same output. Price every task and every skill, and AI exposure becomes a question of dollars. A calibrated measurement instrument for the task-based labor market.

Economics · machine learning · data engineering. A measurement instrument with an open-source engine and a versioned data snapshot.
01

The idea

Think of every industry as a cookbook and its output as the finished meals. A role is a chef assigned a bundle of recipe steps. A task is a single step. A skill is what lets a person perform a step. Government data tells us how many chefs of each kind there are and what they are paid, which steps each performs, and which abilities each step demands.

Push the pay down through those links and nothing is created or destroyed. The same national total appears as industry output, as occupational pay, as the value of tasks, and as the value of skills. Roles, tasks, and skills are three coordinate systems for one object. AI enters as a new appliance that can perform some of the steps. Because every step now carries a price, "which steps can the appliance do" becomes "how many dollars are in play," then "which abilities lose their market," then "which workers are affected and what should they learn next."

02

The model

The dollar shares the identity measures in levels are exactly the calibration a production function needs. A task's expenditure share within an industry is the Cobb-Douglas weight \( \beta_i \). A skill's dollar share of a task's labor is \( \gamma_{ik} \). A task's automatable share maps to \( \delta_i \). Output reads through a nested aggregator of labor and capital:

$$Y = A \cdot R^{\alpha} \, K^{\beta}, \qquad R = \left(\sum_{k} \gamma_k \, s_k^{\,\rho}\right)^{1/\rho}$$

In levels the identity is theory-free arithmetic that conservation makes exact. Theory enters only for counterfactuals: the CES curvature, the skill-to-skill substitutability \( \sigma_{jk} \) built from skill proximity, and the augmentation term \( A \). The structure is an accounting layer that cannot be argued with, and a simulation layer whose every assumption is named, sourced, and swappable. The production system it specifies is the running engine behind these numbers.

03

Findings

01
Every task in America has a price.
The whole structure sits on the published BLS wage bill of $10.70T, reconciled across 820 occupations, 247 industries, 17,266 tasks, and 76 skill elements. Per-occupation means verify against known values (registered nurses $101k, chief executives $276k). Conservation is exact by construction, and every residual is an explicit bucket. The single most valuable task is general managers reviewing performance data, at $31.5B a year.
02
Every ability has a price.
Getting information is a $188B skill, communicating with peers $183B, decision-making $178B, working with computers $168B, and active listening $164B a year.
03
Augmentation now outweighs automation.
At the latest exposure release, $1.40T of task value matches observed AI usage, with a dollar-weighted augmentation share of 44.5 percent against 36.3 percent automation. Usage is not a random draw of task dollars, so this is a measured share rather than a forecast.
04
The exposure measures barely agree, in dollars.
Across the $6.59T covered by all three adapters, capability-based measures correlate 0.73 with each other but near zero with observed usage. Of $1.32T that could be flagged as most exposed, all three agree on just $23B. The consensus is two orders of magnitude smaller than the wage bill, which is why the instrument carries several measures instead of one.
05
The measures load on different margins at different horizons.
Usage-based measures predict where healthcare employment adjusted in the ChatGPT era. A capability-based pre-LLM measure predicts wage growth over the longer window. The employment result passes a pre-ChatGPT placebo cleanly, and the exposure rankings are invariant across 27 calibration settings (minimum correlation 0.9995).

04

What is next

The upgrade that matters most is within-occupation skill differentiation. Today a task's skills are read from its occupation's average profile, which ties the engine to a flat index in validation. Differentiating skills within an occupation is what lets the nested structure predict better than a single number, and it is the clearest test of whether the added machinery earns its place. After that the grain moves from national to state and metro, where workforce boards actually operate, and the credential traversal closes the loop from an exposed skill to the nearby programs that teach its replacement. A second sector beyond healthcare then tests whether the validation result holds outside it.
