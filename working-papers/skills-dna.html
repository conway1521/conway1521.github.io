Research Working paper

AI exposure at three levels

Exposure to generative AI is measured at three different levels of the same labor market, and the measures are then compared as though they described one object. This paper puts them on a common footing, validates the step that carries a task measure down to skills, and reports what the underlying data will bear.

Working paper · fully public data · every number regenerates from a clean checkout.
01

The premise

A firm produces output, that output is produced by activities being carried out, and those activities are carried out by combinations of human capability. Occupations, tasks and skills are three descriptions of one process, so a technology entering production has to be traceable through all three. The premise is common ground rather than a claim of this paper, running from Autor, Levy and Murnane through Acemoglu and Autor, and developed most recently by Freund and Mann into a general equilibrium model in which occupations bundle tasks and automating one task raises the weight on those that remain.

What that literature consumes, and does not examine, are the exposure measures themselves. Felten, Raj and Seamans score the fifty-two abilities of O*NET. Eloundou and co-authors score its task statements. Srinivasan, Chen and Zakerinia score occupations. Each is used at whatever level an application requires, and the aggregation or disaggregation that carries it there has not previously been validated. This paper establishes what each one measures, whether it survives being moved between levels, and what the data underneath will support. Two of the results are cautionary about the measures themselves, and both emerged from reading the construction of a score rather than from analysing its values.

02

Why the levels are not interchangeable

The three layers perform three different functions, and none of them can perform the others. The wage bill is a budget that everything must sum to, which disciplines a description without testing it, because quantities constructed as shares of a total return that total whatever values the shares take. Tasks are where the technology has actually been observed, since every measure of generative AI was built by judging a capability against a description of work. Skills are the only level at which a claim can fail, being fewer in number than the occupations whose wages are observed.

That last point is arithmetic rather than theory, and it has an uncomfortable corollary. There are 18,796 task statements against 829 occupations with observed wages, so a set of task values always fits and can never be contradicted. There are 161 descriptors, and after grouping 25, so a set of skill values is over-determined and can be wrong. Whether a quantity in this literature is testable therefore depends on how many task statements O*NET's analysts happened to write, which is a property of a survey instrument rather than of an economy.

03

What the paper establishes

01
The step from tasks to skills can be validated, and it holds.
Every skill-level result in this literature depends on carrying a task measure down to skills, and the step has not been tested because testing it needs an independent measurement at the destination. That measurement exists. Felten and co-authors measure ability exposure directly; propagating the Eloundou task ratings through the O*NET activity crosswalk reaches the same quantity by a route sharing no data with theirs. Across the fifty-two abilities the two correlate 0.533 by Pearson and 0.632 by Spearman, against a ceiling of 0.591 set by how well Eloundou's own human and machine raters agree with each other. The propagation recovers as much signal as the source contains and adds little in transit.
02
The capability measures are less independent than their agreement suggests.
The Srinivasan automation score correlates 0.833 with Eloundou exposure aggregated to occupations, and the reason is more instructive than convergent validity would be. Their score is the Eloundou construction, applied to the task descriptions of a different O*NET release using a later version of the same model, with the same rubric and the same weighting. The correlation shows that the procedure reproduces across data vintages and model versions, which is worth knowing and is not what agreement between measures is usually taken to show. The field's observation that capability scores agree with one another needs reading in that light, since several of them share a rubric, a source of task descriptions, or both.
03
Augmentation is mostly a transformation of automation, and what is left still carries skill structure.
The augmentation score is not an independent measurement of complementarity. It is defined as one minus a Herfindahl index over the shares of exposed and unexposed tasks in an occupation, on the reasoning that a job mixing automatable work with work that resists automation stands to gain most, which imposes an assumption on the exposure classification rather than observing it a second time. Because that index is a quadratic in the exposed share, a quadratic in the automation score accounts for 77 percent of the variance in augmentation across occupations, so the two dimensions have to be contrasted net of the mechanical part. Doing that changes which half of the contrast stands. Social skills, engineering and technology, arts and humanities, work output and psychomotor abilities hold up, with skill groups explaining 0.209 of the residual variation and eleven of twenty-five groups loading significantly. Business and management and mental processes do not, falling from statistics near three to 0.4 and 1.2, which places the apparent concentration of automation on cognitive and administrative content among the artefacts of the formula rather than among the findings.
04
The before-and-after design cannot answer the question.
Applied to a balanced panel of 693 occupations from 2012 to 2025, no skill group moved by more than 1.51 times its own year-to-year volatility after 2022, and the observed period sits inside the range that relabelling ordinary years produces. The more consequential result is that every deviation from the pre-2020 path begins in 2020 or 2021. On wages the residual runs at -0.005 in 2020 and worsens to -0.025 by 2025; on employment the series takes a -0.040 excursion in 2020 and stays positive from 2021. Large language models arrived in late 2022, after both breaks. The pandemic sits between the pre-period and the treatment period and moves these series by more than the effect being sought, which is a property of the design rather than of this implementation.
05
The instrument constrains what can be concluded.
O*NET importance ratings compress towards uniformity whenever they are converted into shares. In the median occupation the most important task rates 1.41 times the least important, and valuing tasks by that share produces values correlating 0.997 on ranks with dividing an occupation's wage bill by its number of tasks. Descriptors are collinear enough that all 161 predict held-out occupational wages worse than a single measure of required preparation. Comparing any two periods returns between five and nine of twenty-five groups apparently changing course when nothing has happened, so conventional inference overstates significance by a wide margin. O*NET is used anyway, here and elsewhere, because it is free, covers the whole economy, and is the common vocabulary of every measure discussed, whereas the alternatives are restricted individual data that most researchers cannot obtain.
04

What is next

The pandemic moved which occupations grew, whereas a technology of this kind should move what happens inside an occupation, and the second is a different object that a comparison across occupations cannot see. The validated propagation reported here is what a within-occupation design would need, and observed-usage data supplies task-level outcomes on the fifth of tasks it covers, where the selection falls on tasks rather than on time. Separately, the separation of augmentation from automation suggests a shock structure that current models do not represent, since automating a task and assisting it have different implications for who gains, and the loadings reported here are what a model incorporating the distinction would require.
