Open Inequality Atlas: wealth

Wealth inequality data is scattered across mutually incompatible sources: WID, the ECB's HFCS, the Luxembourg Wealth Study, the US SCF, and the Fed's Distributional Financial Accounts. Each uses a different unit, methodology, and license, so a comparable cross-country panel is a research project in itself. My goal with the Atlas was to close that gap into a single, versioned, openly licensed panel of household net-wealth inequality for my own research and for the field. Currently, it houses 10,783 observations across 213 countries from 1800 to 2025, with every row tagged by source priority and comparability tier.

Skills DNA of the economy
task-based labor market, calibrated AI exposure · in progress

Roles, tasks, and skills are three equivalent decompositions of the same output. Price every task and every skill, and AI exposure can become a question of dollars: which steps an application can do, which abilities lose their market, and who is affected.
The idea

Push occupational pay down through the tasks each role performs and the skills each task demands. Nothing is created or destroyed, so the same national total appears as industry output, occupational pay, the value of tasks, and the value of skills. AI enters as an appliance that can perform some priced steps, which turns exposure into a measurable flow of dollars.

The model

$$Y = A \cdot R^{\alpha} \, K^{\beta}, \qquad R = \left(\sum_{k} \gamma_k \, s_k^{\,\rho}\right)^{1/\rho}$$

In levels the identity is theory-free arithmetic. The task shares \( \beta_i \), skill shares \( \gamma_{ik} \), and automatable shares \( \delta_i \) are read straight from the data. The CES curvature and augmentation \( A \) enter only for counterfactuals.

Workforce flow engine
occupational flow parameters · in progress

Every occupation in every state has a metabolism: who enters, who exits, who moves, and a background rate of churn. Standard forecasts give the totals. This measures the rates underneath them from Census microdata, so policy can act on the causes of a workforce change.
The idea

Standard forecasts answer how many. This decomposes an occupation's stock into entry, exit, migration, and residual churn at state by occupation by age band by year. The churn residual is kept as a behavioral parameter, so a state can tell a retention problem from a pipeline problem.

The model

$$\text{Stock}_{t+1} = \text{Stock}_{t} + \text{Entrants} + \text{In-migrants} - \text{Exits} - \text{Out-migrants} + \text{Residual churn}$$

A cohort-component identity on ACS repeated cross-sections. Migration is observed directly, and entry and exit are estimated from age-band dynamics and validated against external records.

Where occupations move
interstate migration by occupation · in progress

We know a lot about the interstate migration of people and little about migration by occupation. Yet occupation is where migration policy bites: nurse licensure compacts, engineer reciprocity, remote-work eligibility. This maps it across 501 occupations and twenty years of census microdata.
The idea

Bilateral state-to-state migration flows by occupation, over 501 codes, 51 jurisdictions, and nineteen vintages of census microdata. An atlas of which occupations move and where, and the wage elasticities of who moves for money. The wide complement to the workforce flow engine.

The model

$$\mathbb{E}\big[\text{flow}_{o \to d}^{\,k}\big] = \exp\!\Big(\eta_k \ln \tfrac{w_d}{w_o} + \beta \ln \text{dist}_{od} + \phi_o + \psi_d\Big)$$

Do skills predict moves?
occupational mobility · in progress

Skill-similarity scores are used as if they were evidence that workers move. In other words, they are a hypothesis about feasibility. This tests whether workers actually move in proportion to similarity, once size, wages, and geography are held constant.
The idea

Estimate a gravity model of occupational flows and read off the elasticity of moves to skill similarity, then check whether the tier thresholds the field uses match where observed flows actually break.

The model

$$\ln \text{flow}_{i \to j} = \eta \, \ln \text{sim}_{ij} + \beta_1 \ln \tfrac{w_j}{w_i} + \beta_2 \ln E_i + \beta_3 \ln E_j + \gamma \, \text{licensed}_j + \varepsilon_{ij}$$

Fiscal policy's distributional impact on consumption
with Paul Hubert and Fergus Cumming · in progress

Identifies the channels through which fiscal policy reaches consumption across households of varying credit constraints, building on Hubert and Cumming (2021) to connect asset liquidity with a fiscal-policy dataset.
The idea

Asks how a fiscal shock reaches consumption differently depending on households' credit constraints and asset liquidity. It pairs a narrative identification of shocks with agent heterogeneity, so a fiscal shock's effect can be measured where it actually lands rather than on average.
